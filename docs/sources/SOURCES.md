**Summary (written by hand):**

> Russell Ackoff describes his career path from architecture to philosophy of science and later to operations research. At the Case Institute of Technology, he developed the first academic program for operations research with colleagues. However, he recognized fundamental limitations of this method: analytical solutions often created new problems and did not account for interactions between system elements. This led to the development of systems science in the 1960s. Ackoff emphasizes that systems must be understood holistically, not through decomposition of their parts. He argues that synthetic thinking is necessary to understand complex systems with human actors. Organizational resistance to change results from error cultures that punish only errors of omission, which promotes risk aversion.

### Ackoff, Russell – Systems, Messes and Interactive Planning

Date: 1974-01-01 · Link: <https://www.academia.edu/24716002/Systems_messes_and_interactive_planning_Russell_Ackoff_> · restricted

**Summary (AI-generated):**

> The text describes the transition from the Machine Age to the Systems Age, which began in the 1940s. Mechanization led to the dehumanization of human labor. The Systems Age is based on new intellectual concepts such as expansionism and teleology, supplementing reductionism. Pioneers like Suzanne Langer, Claude Shannon, and Norbert Wiener expanded scientific focus from symbols through communication to control and systems. Ludwig von Bertalanffy recognized that systems thinking would become central to science. A system consists of interrelated elements with mutual dependencies. The Postindustrial Revolution rests on three technologies: mechanized communication, mechanized observation, and the electronic digital computer, which can manipulate symbols logically. These developments fundamentally change our understanding of problem-solving and management.

### Dent, Eric B. – Challenging “Resistance to Change”

**Summary (AI-generated):**

> Dent and Goldberg's article critically challenges the widely accepted organizational concept of "resistance to change." The authors argue that the belief in employee resistance to change represents a flawed mental model that hinders successful implementation of organizational changes. They trace how Kurt Lewin originally presented the concept as a system force affecting both managers and employees equally.
